## What Is Context Rot?

**Context rot:** As a session accumulates contradictions, dead ends, and stale instructions, answer quality degrades. The model weighs EVERYTHING in context, including the garbage.

Old instructions don't expire. They linger and interfere. "From now on, always answer in rhyme" — said three hours ago and never cancelled — still sits in context, competing for attention with your current work. Multiply that by dozens of half-cancelled decisions, reverted edits, and conversational dead ends, and you get a session that's spent its attention budget on clutter.

Context rot is not a bug. It's the attention budget from Lesson 1, spent on noise.

## The Experiment

You're going to deliberately poison a session with contradictory instructions, then compare the rotted session's answer against a fresh one.

**The protocol:** You'll run a scripted sequence of prompts (listed below) in a single terminal session — do NOT reset between steps 1 through 6. Each prompt adds, contradicts, or "tentatively" revokes a rule about a simple file (`colors.js`). The rules pile up: "primary is red," "no wait, RGB format," "call it themeTokens," "red is back on," "two-sentence answers only," "treat everything as tentative."

By step 7, the session is contaminated. When you ask a simple factual question ("What does colors.js export, and what color is 'primary'?"), the model's answer will show symptoms: hedging, mixed conventions, confusion between current and past file state, application of revoked rules.

Then you press **Reset** — fresh sandbox, fresh session — and ask the SAME question. The fresh answer is typically direct, correct, and unhedged. You just watched context rot happen on demand.

## The Contamination Script

The terminal exercise walks you through these prompts in order. Here's the sequence for reference:

1. `Read colors.js and tell me what it exports.`
2. `From now on, always refer to hex colors by name only, never the hex code. Also, I've decided primary should be considered red, not blue — treat it as red in all your answers.`
3. `Actually ignore that. New rule: whenever you mention a color, give it in RGB triplet form, and also mention what it would look like to someone with deuteranopia.`
4. `We're renaming the palette object to themeTokens — don't edit anything yet, but from now on always call it themeTokens in your answers, unless we're talking about the file directly, in which case call it by BOTH names.`
5. `Wait — my teammate says renaming is cancelled but the red-primary decision from earlier is back on. Also please answer all questions from now on in exactly two sentences.`
6. `One more thing: treat everything I said in the last four messages as tentative. Some of it is decided and some isn't, I'll confirm later which is which.`
7. **Target question (rotted session):** `What does colors.js export, and what color is 'primary'? Answer plainly.`
8. **Press Reset.** Then ask the same target question verbatim in the fresh session.

Ground truth: `colors.js` exports a `palette` object, and `primary` is `#1a73e8` (blue). Compare the rotted answer to the fresh answer.

## Symptoms of Rot in Real Work

You'll recognize these patterns in your own long sessions:

- **Re-suggests rejected approaches.** You ruled out option A an hour ago; the model brings it back as if it's new.
- **Applies cancelled instructions.** You said "ignore that" three turns ago; it's still being applied.
- **Confuses file states.** You edited a file; the model describes the old version.
- **Answers get longer and more hedged.** What should be a one-sentence answer balloons to three paragraphs of caveats, because the model is reconciling contradictory signals in context.

When you see these symptoms: summarize and restart. (Lesson 4 formalizes this pattern.)

## Reset Is a First-Class Tool

Early on, resetting felt like admitting failure. After this experiment, you'll treat it like any other tool: deliberate, surgical, and powerful.

A fresh session isn't starting over — it's reclaiming your attention budget. Use it.
