## What Is Fine-Tuning?

You've learned that **prompting** changes what goes into the context — system instructions, few-shot examples, retrieved documents. The model itself stays the same; only the input changes.

**Fine-tuning** is different. It **continues training** an existing model on your own examples, so the model's internal **weights** shift. You're teaching the model new patterns by adjusting the numbers inside it.

Think of it this way:
- **Prompting** is handing the model a cheat sheet before every question.
- **Fine-tuning** is drilling the model on practice tests until it internalizes the patterns.

Fine-tuning changes **behavior, style, and format** reliably. It's much weaker at adding **facts** — facts belong in RAG, not weights.

<Callout kind="warning">
Fine-tuning for knowledge is like tattooing your calendar on your arm. It's permanent, hard to update, and quickly stale. Use RAG for anything that changes.
</Callout>

## The Ladder: When to Climb Each Rung

When you want to change model behavior, try techniques in this order. Each rung costs roughly **10x the previous** in time, money, and complexity:

### 1. Better Prompt (minutes, free)

Start here. Write a clearer system prompt. Add specific instructions. Define the role. Modules 02–04 taught you this — and you got production-grade behavior from it. The judge that graded you? Pure prompting, no fine-tuning.

**Try this first, always.** Most problems die here.

### 2. Few-Shot Examples (minutes)

If the prompt alone isn't enough, add 2–5 examples of the exact input-output pattern you want. Show, don't just tell. This is still prompting — you're just making the cheat sheet better.

### 3. RAG (days to set up, cheap per call)

If the model needs access to changing knowledge (your docs, your catalog, your support history), build a retrieval index. Chunk, embed, store. At call time, retrieve and stuff the prompt.

RAG is for **facts you can't bake into the model**. It updates instantly when your documents change.

### 4. Fine-Tuning (weeks, ongoing retraining cost)

Only climb to this rung when the first three can't solve it. Fine-tuning requires:

- **A training dataset** of hundreds or thousands of labeled examples
- **Evaluation** to measure improvement
- **Retraining** every time the base model upgrades (and they upgrade often)
- **Weeks of iteration** to get it right

Fine-tuning makes sense when you need:
- **Locked output formats** at massive scale, where every prompt token counts
- **Consistent brand voice** that prompting can't reliably hold
- **Latency/cost savings** by replacing a large prompted model with a smaller fine-tuned one

But for most teams, **most of the time, the ladder stops at rung 2 or 3**.

## When Fine-Tuning Is Wrong

Here's a quick test: if the answer is "it depends on this week's data," **don't fine-tune.**

### Example: Product Catalog

Your company's product catalog changes every week. You want the AI to answer customer questions about it.

- ❌ **Fine-tune the model on the catalog every week.** Expensive, slow, and you're always a week behind.
- ✅ **RAG: index the catalog and retrieve relevant entries per question.** Update the index in seconds when the catalog changes.

Fine-tuning bakes knowledge into weights. Weights are the wrong place for anything that changes. Facts belong in retrieval.

### Example: Tone Is Inconsistent

A teammate says, "Our AI's tone is all over the place. Let's fine-tune!"

- ❌ **Jump to fine-tuning** without trying cheaper options.
- ✅ **Climb the ladder**: improve the system prompt ("You are professional, concise, and warm — never sarcastic or robotic"). Add 2–3 few-shot examples of the desired tone. Test.

Tone is often fixable with **prompting alone** — the same techniques you practiced in modules 02–04. Fine-tuning is a last resort, not a first move.

## When Fine-Tuning Wins

Fine-tuning genuinely shines for:

1. **Strict output formats across millions of calls.** You need JSON with a specific schema, every time, and you're making so many calls that shaving 50 tokens off the system prompt saves thousands of dollars.
2. **Brand voice that prompting can't lock down.** You've tried better prompts and few-shot examples, and the model still drifts. Fine-tuning on hundreds of on-brand examples can internalize the style.
3. **Model compression.** You're using a big, expensive model and discover that a smaller, cheaper one — fine-tuned on your task — performs just as well. Latency and cost both drop.

If your problem is **not** one of these, the answer is probably not fine-tuning.

## What You've Learned

Fine-tuning changes the model's weights; prompting changes the input. Fine-tuning is powerful for behavior and style but expensive and slow to iterate. Follow the ladder: better prompt → few-shot → RAG → fine-tune, in that order. Each rung costs 10x the last. Most teams never need the top rung. Facts belong in RAG, not weights — anything that changes weekly is the wrong fit for fine-tuning.

You've now seen the four core techniques every AI product uses: **prompting, tool use, RAG, and fine-tuning**. Next, the last missing piece: cost.
