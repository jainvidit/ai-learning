# Randomness & Why Answers Differ

Ask an AI chatbot the same question today and tomorrow, and you may get two noticeably different answers. Neither is a glitch. Neither means the model "changed its mind." A little randomness is *built in* — on purpose.

Let's see where it comes from, and how to turn it up or down.

## A menu, not a single answer

Remember: at every step, the model is choosing the next word (technically, the next *token* — see Lesson 2). But here's the part we haven't stressed yet: it doesn't compute *one* next word. It computes a whole **menu** of candidates, each with a probability — a score for how likely that word is to come next.

Say the text so far is *"The cat sat on the..."* The menu might look like:

- **mat** — 60%
- **couch** — 20%
- **windowsill** — 10%
- **roof** — 5%
- ...and hundreds of longer shots

Here's the surprise: the model doesn't always pick the #1 option. Instead, it *rolls weighted dice* over the menu. "mat" wins most of the time, but every so often "windowsill" gets its moment. And since each chosen word becomes part of the text that shapes the *next* menu, one different early pick can send the whole answer down a different path — like taking a different first turn out of your driveway and ending up on a completely different route.

That's the whole mystery of "why did I get a different answer today?" Different dice rolls, different paths.

## The temperature dial

How adventurous those dice rolls are is controlled by a setting called **temperature**. Don't let the physics-y name scare you — it's just a dial from "play it safe" to "surprise me."

- **Low temperature** → the model almost always picks the top choice. Answers become predictable, repeatable, and a bit plain. Ask twice, get nearly the same thing.
- **High temperature** → lower-ranked words get picked more often. Answers become varied, creative, occasionally weird. Ask twice, get two genuinely different responses.

Think of a jazz musician. Low temperature is playing the sheet music exactly as written — reliable, identical every night. High temperature is improvising around the melody — delightful, and never the same twice.

<Callout kind="info">
Most chat apps run at a moderate temperature by default — enough spice that responses feel natural and don't repeat word-for-word. You usually can't see the dial, but it's there, quietly rolling dice on every word.
</Callout>

## When you WANT randomness — and when you don't

Randomness isn't a flaw to eliminate. It's a tool to match to the job.

**You want it high (variety) when:**

- Brainstorming names, ideas, or angles — you *want* ten different options
- Creative writing — a poem that's identical every time is a poster, not a poem
- Rephrasing something that sounds stale

**You want it low (consistency) when:**

- Asking factual questions — there's one right answer; creativity just adds ways to be wrong
- Generating code or step-by-step instructions — "adventurous" is not a compliment for either
- Anything you'll compare, reuse, or automate — you need the same input to give the same output

## No dial? Use your words

In most chat apps you can't touch the temperature setting directly. Good news: **your prompt can do the dial's job.**

To push toward consistency, ask for it explicitly:

> "Give the single most standard, conventional answer. Don't be creative. Format it exactly as: Name: ... / Slogan: ..."

Words like *standard*, *conventional*, and *most common*, plus a strict output format, make the safe choice the plausible choice — so the dice matter less. To push the other way, invite variety: *"Give me 10 wildly different options, from safe to strange."*

<Callout kind="tip">
When answers keep wandering, add two things to your prompt: **"choose the most conventional option"** and **an exact output format**. Constraint is the beginner's temperature dial.
</Callout>

## The takeaway

Every answer is a chain of weighted dice rolls over a menu of likely next words. The temperature dial sets how daring the rolls are — and when you can't reach the dial, precise wording ("give the single most standard answer, in this exact format") does the same job. Match the randomness to the task: dial it up to explore, down to rely.
