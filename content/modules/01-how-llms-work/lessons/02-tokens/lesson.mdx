## The model doesn't see what you see

In the last lesson, we said an LLM predicts "the next word." That was a friendly simplification, and now it's time to meet the real unit: the **token**.

When you read a sentence, you see letters that form words. An LLM sees neither. Before your message ever reaches the model, a program called a **tokenizer** chops it into chunks — and those chunks, called tokens, are all the model ever sees.

Think of it like a chef's meal kit. You might think of a recipe in terms of "dinner," but the kit arrives as pre-portioned packets: one packet of rice, one of sauce, two of chopped vegetables. Tokens are the pre-portioned packets of language.

## Chunks, not words

Here's the key rule of thumb:

- **Common words become a single token.** Words like "the," "cat," "coffee," and "running" appear so often in text that the tokenizer gives each one its own packet.
- **Rare or unusual words get split into pieces.** A word like "antidisestablishmentarianism" almost never appears in text, so the tokenizer breaks it into several smaller chunks it *does* recognize — something like "anti / dis / establishment / arian / ism."

Why do it this way? Efficiency. The tokenizer keeps a fixed menu of chunks (roughly a hundred thousand of them) that can be combined to spell out *anything* — every word, name, emoji, and typo ever written. Common things get short codes; rare things get assembled from parts. It's the same logic as shorthand: frequent phrases get their own symbol, unusual ones get spelled out.

<Callout kind="info">
A handy rough conversion for English: **one token is about three-quarters of a word**, or around 4 characters. So 100 tokens is roughly 75 words. You'll see this ratio come up whenever people talk about AI pricing or limits.
</Callout>

## The strawberry problem

Here's a famous puzzle: ask an AI *"How many r's are in the word strawberry?"* and it may confidently answer "two." (The right answer is three: st**r**awbe**rr**y.)

How can something that writes beautiful essays fail a task a seven-year-old can do? Tokens.

To the model, "strawberry" isn't ten letters. It's one or two tokens — sealed packets. The model sees the packet labeled *strawberry*, not the letters inside it. Asking it to count the r's is like asking someone to count the grains of rice in a sealed meal-kit packet by looking at the label. It can guess from experience, but it can't actually see inside.

<Callout kind="warning">
This is why LLMs are often shaky at letter-level tasks: counting letters, reversing words, precise rhyming, or acrostics. It's not that the model is dumb — it literally reads at a different resolution than you do. When an AI stumbles on something like this, you now know why.
</Callout>

## Tokens are also the currency

One more reason tokens matter: they're how AI usage is *measured*. Everything an LLM reads and writes is counted in tokens, and every model has a limit on how many tokens it can handle in one conversation. That limit is called the **context window** — think of it as the model's desk space, or a token budget for the whole conversation.

If your conversation gets longer than the budget, the oldest parts fall off the desk, and the model genuinely can't see them anymore. Ever had a long chat where the AI seemed to forget what you said at the beginning? That's the context window at work.

<Callout kind="tip">
We're only teasing the context window here — a later module digs into how big these budgets are, what happens when you hit them, and how to work within them. For now, just remember: **tokens in, tokens out, and there's a budget.**
</Callout>

## The takeaway

Last lesson's mental model gets a small upgrade: an LLM is a machine that predicts the next **token**, over and over. It reads and writes in chunks — not letters, not quite words — and that one fact explains its odd blind spots (strawberry!), how its usage is measured, and why conversations have a size limit.
