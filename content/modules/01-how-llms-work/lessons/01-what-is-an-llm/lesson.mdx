## Start with your phone

Pick up your phone and start typing a text message: "I'm running a bit..."

Before you finish, your keyboard offers a suggestion: **late**. Maybe also **behind** and **low**. Your phone isn't reading your mind. It has simply seen the phrase "I'm running a bit late" so many times that it knows *late* is the most likely next word.

That little grey suggestion above your keyboard is the seed of everything in this course. Hold onto it.

## An LLM is that idea, taken to the extreme

An **LLM** — short for **large language model** — is a computer program that does one thing: given some text, it predicts what word is most likely to come next.

That's it. That's the whole trick.

"But wait," you might say, "ChatGPT writes essays and answers questions. That's more than suggesting one word!"

Here's the secret: it does the one-word trick *repeatedly*. It predicts a word, adds it to the text, then predicts the next word after that, and the next, and the next — like laying down one railroad tie at a time until there's a whole track. A full paragraph is just thousands of tiny "what comes next?" predictions strung together.

<Callout kind="info">
A quick word on words: the model actually predicts small chunks of text called *tokens*, which are sometimes whole words and sometimes pieces of words. We'll meet tokens properly in the next lesson. For now, "predicts the next word" is close enough to the truth.
</Callout>

## Why does it feel so smart, then?

Your phone's autocomplete is trained on a modest amount of text and makes fairly simple guesses. An LLM is trained on a staggering amount of text — think of a library so large it would take you thousands of lifetimes to read — and it uses billions of internal dials (we'll see how those get set in lesson 3) to make its guesses.

Here's the surprising part: when next-word prediction gets *that* good, it starts to look like understanding.

Think about what it takes to correctly finish this sentence: "The capital of France is ___." To reliably predict *Paris*, the model has to have soaked up facts about geography. To finish "She was furious, so she slammed the ___," it helps to have absorbed patterns about human emotions and doors. Really good prediction quietly requires a kind of knowledge.

So scale is the magic ingredient. A small predictor feels like autocomplete. An enormous one feels like a conversation partner. But under the hood, it's the same move: *what word probably comes next?*

## What an LLM is *not*

This is the most important section of the lesson, because our brains love to imagine a little person inside the machine. There isn't one. Let's clear up three common misunderstandings.

**It doesn't look things up in a database.** When you ask an LLM a question, it doesn't search a filing cabinet of stored facts and quotes. Everything it "knows" is baked into those billions of dials as *patterns* — more like how you remember the gist of a book you read years ago than like finding the exact page.

**It doesn't have beliefs or opinions.** When an LLM writes "I think the beach is the best vacation spot," it isn't sharing a preference. It's producing text that is a *likely continuation* of the conversation so far. Ask it the same thing framed differently and it might happily argue for the mountains.

**It doesn't have goals or plans of its own.** It isn't sitting there between your messages, thinking. It only does anything when given text to continue — like a player piano that's silent until someone feeds in a roll of music.

<Callout kind="warning">
Because an LLM produces *likely* text rather than *verified* text, it can produce sentences that sound confident and correct but are simply wrong. This is called hallucination, and it gets its own lesson later in this module.
</Callout>

## So where do ChatGPT and Claude fit in?

Names like **ChatGPT** (made by OpenAI) and **Claude** (made by Anthropic) are products built *around* LLMs. Think of the LLM as a powerful engine, and the chat app as the car built around it: there's a friendly interface, safety training to make the model helpful and polite (more on that in lesson 3), and extra tools bolted on.

So when someone says "I asked the AI," what actually happened is: their words went to a very large next-word predictor, which generated a likely, helpful continuation, one small piece at a time.

<Callout kind="tip">
Keep this one-sentence mental model in your pocket for the rest of the course: **an LLM is a machine that predicts the next word, repeated over and over, trained on a huge amount of text.** Every lesson that follows — tokens, training, hallucination, randomness — is just a closer look at a different part of that sentence.
</Callout>
