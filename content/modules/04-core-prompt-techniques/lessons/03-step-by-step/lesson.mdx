Back in Module 02, you learned how to iterate on answers that aren't quite right. Here's why the model's FIRST answer to a tricky question is often wrong: it tried to jump straight to the conclusion.

## The stepping-stone effect

Remember from Module 01: the model produces one token at a time, and each token can only build on what's already written. If the final answer appears immediately in the first sentence, there's nothing supporting it — it's a blind leap.

Writing intermediate steps first gives later tokens solid ground to stand on. Each conclusion rests on visible reasoning rather than thin air.

Think of it like crossing a river. You can try to leap the whole span in one jump (risky), or you can place stepping stones and walk across confidently. Intermediate steps are those stones.

## The technique: ask for it

The simplest version is literal: "Think through this step by step before answering."

That's often enough. The model will show its work, then give the final answer.

For extra clarity, combine this with the XML structure you just learned:

```
Show your reasoning in <thinking> tags, then give the final answer in <answer> tags.
```

Now the steps are labeled and separated. The structure makes it easy to find exactly what you need — the final answer is always in the `<answer>` section.

## Example: a tricky calculation

Here's a prompt without step-by-step reasoning:

```
A gym membership costs $60/month with a $100 joining fee.
Another gym costs $70/month with no joining fee.
Which is cheaper over a year?
```

The model might jump to an answer: "The second one is cheaper." But wait — is that true? Over 12 months, the first gym costs $100 + ($60 × 12) = $820. The second costs $70 × 12 = $840. The first one is actually cheaper.

Without steps, the model guessed wrong.

Now try this version:

```
A gym membership costs $60/month with a $100 joining fee.
Another gym costs $70/month with no joining fee.
Which is cheaper over a year?

Show your reasoning in <thinking> tags, then give the final answer in <answer> tags.
```

With this structure, the model writes something like:

```
<thinking>
First gym: $100 joining fee + $60/month for 12 months = $100 + $720 = $820
Second gym: $70/month for 12 months = $840
$820 < $840, so the first gym is cheaper.
</thinking>

<answer>
The first gym is cheaper. It costs $820 for the year compared to $840 for the second gym.
</answer>
```

Each step is visible. The math is shown. The final answer is clearly separated and correct.

## Second benefit: auditability

Visible reasoning doesn't just improve accuracy — it lets YOU catch errors.

If the model makes a mistake, you can see exactly which step went wrong and give surgical feedback:

> "Step 3 assumes the discount applies twice — it only applies once. Redo from step 3."

That's precise iteration, not vague "try again" requests. You learned iteration in Module 02; visible steps make iteration far more effective.

## When to use step-by-step reasoning

This technique shines on:

- **Math and calculations**: budgets, pricing comparisons, anything with numbers
- **Planning**: scheduling tasks around constraints, comparing options with multiple factors
- **Multi-step logic**: "If A, and if B depends on A, then what?"

Skip it for:

- **Simple recall**: "What year was the treaty signed?" Either the model knows it or it doesn't; steps won't help.
- **Creative writing**: a haiku doesn't need derivation — steps just add clutter.

Use step-by-step thinking when the answer is CONSTRUCTED from reasoning, not recalled or invented.

<Callout kind="info">
Newer models can sometimes reason internally on their own — you might hear this called "extended thinking" or similar features. You'll explore that properly in a later module. Explicitly requesting visible steps still helps when you want to SEE and AUDIT the reasoning, which is half the value.
</Callout>

## Recap

- Ask the model to "think step by step" before answering
- Use `<thinking>` and `<answer>` tags to separate reasoning from the final result
- Visible steps make answers more accurate AND let you spot and fix errors surgically
- Use it for constructed answers (math, planning, logic), not recall or creativity
