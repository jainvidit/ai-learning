Your prompts are getting more sophisticated. You're including background context, specific instructions, pasted emails to rewrite, and few-shot examples. But when you paste everything together, a problem appears: where does the email end and the instruction begin?

Sometimes the model rewrites your INSTRUCTIONS instead of the email you wanted it to rewrite. The boundaries blur. You need labeled boxes.

## XML tags: labeled envelopes for your content

The solution is simple: wrap each section in **XML tags**. These are just labels in angle brackets around a section of text:

```
<email>
Dear Customer,
We regret to inform you...
</email>
```

An opening tag (`<email>`), the content, and a closing tag with a slash (`</email>`). You can name them anything sensible — `<instructions>`, `<context>`, `<document>`, `<notes>`, whatever fits the content.

No programming knowledge required. Think of tags as labeled envelopes: you're just putting different pieces of your prompt into labeled containers so they don't get mixed up.

## Why it works

Language models saw enormous amounts of tagged text during training — HTML, XML, markdown code blocks — and learned to treat tags as strong section boundaries. Claude in particular is trained to respect them carefully.

When you write:

```
<instructions>
Rewrite the email below to be friendlier.
</instructions>

<email>
Your account has been suspended.
</email>
```

The model sees two clearly labeled boxes. It knows "Your account has been suspended" is the email to transform, not part of the instructions themselves.

## The classic layout

Here's a proven structure for complex prompts:

```
<instructions>
Your task and rules go here.
</instructions>

<context>
Background information the model needs.
</context>

<document>
The email, notes, or text you're asking the model to work with.
</document>

<examples>
<example>
Input: ...
Output: ...
</example>

<example>
Input: ...
Output: ...
</example>
</examples>
```

Name each section by its content: `<email>` for emails, `<meeting_notes>` for notes, `<code>` for code. Descriptive names help both you and the model track what's what.

## Power move: cross-references

Once you've tagged your sections, you can refer to them by name in your instructions:

```
<instructions>
Rewrite the text in <email> following the tone shown in <examples>.
Make it friendlier but keep all the details from <context>.
</instructions>

<context>
The customer paid on time. This is an automatic notice.
</context>

<email>
Your account has been flagged for review.
</email>

<examples>
Before: Payment overdue.
After: Just a friendly reminder about your payment.
</examples>
```

The cross-references — "the text in `<email>`", "the tone shown in `<examples>`" — remove the last bit of ambiguity. The model knows exactly which piece plays which role.

<Callout kind="tip">
Any time a prompt contains pasted material longer than a couple of lines, wrap it in a tag. It's the cheapest reliability upgrade in prompting.
</Callout>

## Quick rules

- **Every opening tag needs a closing tag**: `<email>` must have `</email>` (note the slash).
- **Tag sections, not sentences**: Don't wrap every individual sentence. Tag meaningful chunks — the instructions as one section, the document as another.
- **Descriptive names beat generic ones**: `<email>` tells the model what it contains; `<section1>` doesn't.
- **Nesting is fine**: You can put `<example>` tags inside an `<examples>` section if it helps organize.

That's it. Tags are labeled envelopes. Use them to keep your prompt organized and unambiguous.
