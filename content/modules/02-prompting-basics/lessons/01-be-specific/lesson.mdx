Imagine you text a friend: "get me food."

What do you get? Maybe a burger, maybe pizza, maybe something you're allergic to. Now imagine instead you text: "get me a medium veggie burrito from the place on 5th, no onions." Same friend, wildly different odds of getting what you want.

AI is the same — except it won't even ask follow-up questions unless you invite it to.

## Why vague prompts produce generic answers

Remember from the last module: the model predicts likely continuations. When your prompt is vague — like "write about dogs" — thousands of different continuations are equally plausible. The model doesn't know if you want a story, a guide, a poem, or facts. So it lands on the statistical average of all those possibilities: generic, safe, forgettable text.

Specificity narrows the prediction space. Instead of thousands of likely next words, you give the model a clear target. The more you specify, the less the model has to guess.

## Anatomy of a specific prompt

A good prompt includes three things:

1. **The task** — what you want the model to do
2. **The audience** — who it's for
3. **Constraints** — length, tone, what to include or avoid

Here's the difference:

**Vague:** "write about dogs"

**Specific:** "Write 3 tips for first-time dog owners in an apartment, friendly tone, under 100 words."

The second version gives the model a clear job. It knows the task (tips, not a story), the audience (first-timers in an apartment), and the constraints (3 tips, friendly, short).

<Callout kind="tip">
A good self-check: could two reasonable people read your prompt and produce very different things? If yes, it's too vague.
</Callout>

## The real cost of vagueness

You don't save time with a short prompt. You spend it reading an answer you can't use, then rewriting your request anyway. Specificity up front is faster.

The model is ready to give you exactly what you ask for — but you have to ask.
